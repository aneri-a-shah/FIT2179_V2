Where Australians are going - and how that has changed

The Australian travel landscape has undergone profound recalibration between 2019 and 2025, a period defined not just by the challenges of a global shutdown, but by a radical transformation in how, where, and why we travel. From the quiet empty terminals of 2020 to the surging, record-breaking demand of 2025, the industry has seen a dynamic shift in both international flight paths and domestic holiday preferences.

This dashboard visualises the volatility and resilience of this recovery. We look beyond surface-level figures to identify how specific destinations, ranging from established local coastal hubs to rapidly emerging international markets, have ascended and declined in prominence. By mapping this transition, we uncover the hidden "engine" of the domestic market: the non-discretionary travel driven by human connection, the economic "buffer" of day-trip volume, and the shifting spending habits of a consumer base facing new financial pressures.

This analysis provides a critical perspective for modern tourism operators, urban planners, and policy-makers. By understanding these shifting consumer budgets, stakeholders can better anticipate future volatility and implement proactive recovery strategies in an increasingly competitive, and evolving, global market.

Exploring the International Hotspots

Australian outbound travel is heavily dictated by geographic convenience, the invisible tether of distance and flight time. It is clear that destinations like Indonesia and New Zealand remain the primary magnets for Australian travelers. Their dominance in our data highlights a fundamental truth: for the average holidaymaker, the ease of a short-haul transit is the ultimate deciding factor.

However, the map tells a two-speed story. While the Asia-Pacific region accounts for the highest sheer volume of visitors, the persistent, long-reaching lines to Europe and the Americas reveal a different aspiration. Despite the barriers of cost and time, Australians continue to treat these destinations as the quintessential "trip of a lifetime," proving that desire often outweighs the inconvenience of a long-haul flight.

The Aussie Dollar vs the World

The data reveals a high degree of price sensitivity in Australian outbound travel. Destinations located in the top-right quadrant, most notably Japan, demonstrate that a favorable exchange rate acts as a powerful catalyst for tourism growth, effectively lowering the barrier to entry for many Australian holidaymakers.

Conversely, the bottom-left quadrant tells a cautionary story of economic friction. The sustained strength of the US dollar against the Australian dollar has created a tangible cooling effect on travel volumes. This illustrates that even established, "legacy" holiday destinations are not immune to currency pressures. When the financial cost of a trip becomes too volatile, travelers are quick to pivot toward more budget-friendly alternatives.

COVID-19 Tourism Effects and Recovery

Post-COVID travel didn't simply return to the status quo, it fundamentally recalibrated. The extended periods of lockdown between 2020 and 2021 altered the Australian traveler's psychology, fueling a pent-up demand for exploration. The dramatic surge in popularity for destinations like Japan (233% increase) and Vietnam (199% increase) illustrates a strategic redirection of Australian tourism capital toward East and South-East Asia, rather than a uniform global recovery.

In stark contrast, destinations lingering in the red zone, such as the United States, highlight the reality of modern travel barriers. Whether due to the compounding pressure of inflation, unfavourable exchange rates, changing political climates or volatile flight connectivity, these regions are finding it difficult to reclaim their 2019 baseline. This disparity suggests that the "recovery" is not merely a matter of time, but a result of shifting cultural interests and changing economic realities.

Domestic Travel: Where Aussies Holiday at Home

This reveals that domestic tourism is overwhelmingly anchored to the eastern coastline, particularly across Queensland, New South Wales, and Victoria. This concentration underscores a heavy reliance on established coastal infrastructure and the high-density economic engines of our major capital cities.

In stark contrast, the relative emptiness of Central Australia, the Northern Territory, and Western Australia emphasises a significant divide. For the average Australian traveler, "domestic holiday" is synonymous with coastal access. This positions the outback and rural interior as a distinct, niche tourism market, operating quite separately from the high-volume traffic of the East Coast.

Battle of the States

The national tourism landscape is defined by the Big Three heavyweights: New South Wales, Queensland, and Victoria. Together, they dominate the market share, consistently attracting the highest volume of international visitors and setting the tempo for the broader industry.

However, the smaller states and territories demonstrate remarkable resilience. Despite starting with a significantly smaller footprint of international traffic, every region has staged a near-total recovery to pre-2019 baseline levels. This proves that while the Big Three control the volume, the entire Australian tourism ecosystem has effectively neutralised the shock of the pandemic.

Day Trips vs Overnighters: Aussies on a Budget

Overnight trips serve as the incredibly stable baseline of the domestic tourism economy, showing year-over-year consistency. In contrast, day trips display a volatile trend, acting as a crucial economic buffer. As cost-of-living pressures intensified throughout 2025, many Australian travelers seemingly substituted expensive, multi-day holidays for more budget-friendly, high-volume day trips.

We also identify a significant "logistical tax." States with the widest cost gap, the Northern Territory, Western Australia, and Tasmania, are geographically isolated, requiring more complex transit. This forces overnight spend to skyrocket compared to the highly competitive, accessible markets in Victoria and New South Wales, where infrastructure and competition drive lower consumer costs.

Breaking Down the Budget

The data highlights that Transport and Food & Drink are the undisputed anchors of the Australian travel wallet, consistently consuming over half of the total budget year-round. These are the "non-negotiables", the essential costs that must be cleared before a traveler can even consider the rest of their itinerary.

Because these essential pillars, alongside Accommodation, command such a massive collective share, the remaining budget for Activities & Other is remarkably slim. This reveals a critical market reality: tour operators and experience providers are not just competing with each other, they are fighting for the smallest, yet most highly contested fraction of the traveler's remaining budget.

The Purpose of the Trip

While traditional Holiday travel holds the top spot for why Australians venture away from home, the sheer volume of travel for Visiting Friends and Relatives reveals the "hidden engine" of our domestic market.

This data highlights that a massive segment of Australian travel is effectively non-discretionary, it is predominantly driven by human connection rather than pure leisure. This segment acts as a vital, resilient economic floor, because unlike purely discretionary holiday spending, this travel persists even when economic conditions tighten, providing a crucial stabilising force for the entire tourism ecosystem.
